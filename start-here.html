Alignment Theory studies how adaptive systems preserve coherent agency under constraint.

Michael Nathan Bower · Updated September 27, 2026 · Working conceptual and design framework; not peer reviewed.

Coherent agency under constraint

Alignment is the preservation of coherent agency under constraint. The framework asks how people, institutions, and artificial systems retain the capacity to understand, judge, refuse, revise, act, learn, and remain accountable as demands change.

A system may produce good results while losing those capacities. It may also preserve nominal choice while leaving a person too rushed, dependent, or poorly informed to change the outcome.

Capacity and participation

A capacity-forming function is an activity in which continued participation helps maintain the ability to perform, judge, regulate, or adapt. Remembering, deciding, practicing, and correcting are examples. Participatory capacity is the retained ability to remain meaningfully involved in these functions.

Capacity can be shared across people, tools, and institutions. Healthy functioning can require continuing support.

Support and substitution

Support preserves or deepens participation in capacity-forming functions. Substitution carries a function in the participant's place in a way that can preserve immediate output while weakening longer-term participation or capacity.

The same tool may scaffold one task and substitute in another. Consider the person's capacity, how often the tool is used, whether mistakes can be reversed, and who still understands and directs the work.

The four modes remain constitutive co-regulation, developmental scaffolding, stable distributed competence, and substitutive dependence. Support/substitution is a mechanism within the broader theory.

Constraints and usable agency

Constraints shape the space in which action is possible. A boundary can make a task intelligible and safe; an arbitrary or deceptive boundary can capture agency. More options alone do not guarantee more usable freedom.

A review screen preserves agency only if the person has sufficient context, time, comprehension, and a practical ability to refuse or revise the action. Read Constraint, Agency, and Alignment for the evaluation questions.

Authority and continuity

Authority determines who may legitimately bind or delegate action, within what scope and for how long. A true gate gives rightful authority practical power to halt, revise, refuse, approve, or escalate while the outcome can still change.

Semantic continuity asks whether meaning, sources, uncertainty, and context survive a handoff. Runtime continuity asks whether the action that executes is the action that was authorized. A fluent summary can distort its source; an approved plan can also change at execution.

Governance and internalization

Healthy governance preserves or restores meaningful agency within legitimate boundaries. Internalization describes how understanding, skill, judgment, and governed habit increasingly carry what first required external checks.

Where capacity and accountability mature, unnecessary gating can become lighter. Continuing support and legitimate permanent constraints may still be needed. In AGS, Governance Memory recommends changes from receipt history for human review; it cannot silently rewrite policy or authority.

Two applications of the theory

Human Agency Infrastructure (HAPI) applies the theory to human systems, institutions, products, and public life. It asks whether people can understand, refuse, revise, direct action, and recover agency over time.

Alignment Governance Stack (AGS) applies it to delegated operational agency in agentic AI. Human or organizational responsibility remains upstream. Its operator-facing trajectory connects authority, context, reasoning, gates, execution checks, receipts, memory, and the Continuity Console.

Current research

Cultivated Alignment tests whether repeated governed experience can produce stronger self-governance. Its tail-preserving research direction asks whether rare cases, truthful dissent, provenance, and useful capability survive as intervention decreases. The research asks whether governance-consistent behavior persists as external support is progressively reduced. Model/policy internalization has not been demonstrated here.

History and interpretive scope

The earlier internal/external framing distinguished integrated regulation from externally imposed compliance. The May 2026 model developed participation and the four modes of support. The September synthesis brings constraints, authority, continuity, governance, and internalization into the public center.

Cross-domain comparisons suggest questions to test; they do not establish that people, institutions, and artificial systems work identically. Biblical and Babel readings are a separate philosophical/theological interpretation, not scientific proof or a prerequisite for understanding the theory.
